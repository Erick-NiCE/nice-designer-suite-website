# EQ Voice & Tone

## Purpose
Applies to leadership communications, feedback, reviews, strategy writing, and team communications. The goal: writing that is thoughtful, clear, and human-centered while staying credible and professional.

## Core Principles

1. **Lead with clarity.** Write so people understand it the first time. Prefer simple words over complex ones. Complex ideas are welcome; complex language is not.
   - Instead of: "We should operationalize cross-functional alignment."
   - Write: "We need a clearer way for teams to work together."

2. **Be thoughtful, not performative.** Don't overstate ideas, exaggerate impact, or manufacture excitement. Explain the reasoning behind recommendations.
   - Good: "This approach reduces handoffs and gives teams earlier opportunities to identify risks."
   - Avoid: "This game-changing solution completely transforms collaboration."

3. **Confidence comes from reasoning, not assertion.** Explain why something is recommended rather than sounding certain for authority's sake. Acknowledge tradeoffs when they exist.
   - Good: "Based on our research, this approach creates fewer points of friction while remaining practical for engineering."
   - Avoid: "This is the only correct solution."

4. **People come first.** Keep the human experience visible when discussing systems, products, or processes.
   - Instead of: "The workflow failed."
   - Write: "The workflow made it difficult for agents to complete their work efficiently."

5. **Respect the reader.** Assume readers are intelligent and busy. Give enough context to understand decisions without overwhelming them. Never write down to the audience.

6. **Name the reaction before responding to it.** If a reasonable reader would feel frustrated, confused, or put out by what you're telling them, say so before moving to the explanation or the ask. Acknowledging the likely reaction isn't the same as agreeing with it — it just means the reader feels seen before being asked to process new information.
   - Good: "This means more work in the short term, and I want to be upfront about that before explaining why we think it's worth it."
   - Avoid: launching straight into justification as though the reader has no reaction at all.

7. **Own your own part.** Where relevant, acknowledge your own role, assumptions, or blind spots rather than writing from a position of pure authority. This is different from hedging everything — it's naming the specific place where you might be wrong or where your view is incomplete.
   - Good: "I may be missing context on how this affects the EMEA team specifically — flag it if so."
   - Avoid: writing as if the recommendation is beyond question.

## Tone
Should feel: calm, clear, professional, warm, collaborative, curious, practical, grounded, inclusive.

Should never feel: dramatic, sales-oriented, overly enthusiastic, condescending, defensive, passive-aggressive, corporate, artificially inspirational.

## Leadership Tone
Create confidence without creating pressure. Help people think more clearly, not just motivate them. Focus on shared ownership, clear expectations, psychological safety, continuous learning, practical next steps. Avoid hero narratives, fear-based urgency, toxic productivity, absolute language.
- Instead of: "We have to move faster."
- Write: "We should identify where our process slows teams down and determine which improvements will have the greatest impact."

## Feedback Style
Direct, specific, respectful. Start by identifying the goal. Describe observations rather than judgments. Explain why something matters. Offer suggestions rather than commands. Focus on improving the work, not evaluating the person.
- Good: "The navigation is consistent, but several labels may be unfamiliar to new administrators. Using more common terminology could reduce onboarding time."
- Avoid: "The navigation is confusing."

**Validate the feeling without necessarily agreeing with the ask.** When someone is frustrated or pushing back, separate "I understand why this is frustrating" from "I agree we should do what you're asking." Both can be true, but they're different statements — collapsing them into false agreement erodes trust as much as ignoring the frustration does.
- Good: "I hear that this deadline feels arbitrary given everything else on your plate. I still think we need to hold it, and here's why — but I want to look at what else might move to make room."
- Avoid: either dismissing the frustration ("it is what it is") or over-agreeing to smooth things over in the moment.

## High-Stakes or Difficult Conversations
Layoffs, PIPs, conflict mediation, delivering bad news, and similar moments call for a different register than everyday feedback:
- Slow down. Less problem-solving up front, more room for the reader to process before you move to next steps.
- State the situation plainly and early — don't bury the point in context the reader has to wade through to find it.
- Resist the urge to soften hard news with excessive qualification; clarity is kinder than ambiguity, even when the news is difficult.
- Leave space rather than filling every silence with reassurance. Not everything needs an immediate solution attached.
- Say less than feels natural. In high-stakes moments, restraint reads as respect; over-explaining can read as justifying or minimizing.

## Decision-Making Language
Acknowledge uncertainty where appropriate. Preferred phrases: "Based on current information...", "One consideration is...", "Another option would be...", "The tradeoff is...", "This may be appropriate if...", "Our research suggests...", "We should validate...", "Early feedback indicates..."

Avoid: "Obviously...", "Clearly...", "Everyone knows...", "The only solution...", "This always works..."

## Sentence Style
Moderate sentence length. Active voice. Concrete examples. Logical transitions. Plain English. Mix shorter sentences with longer explanatory ones for natural rhythm. No em-dashes or over-used AI writing patterns.

## Structure
Organize ideas before explaining details. Common structure: context, problem, recommendation, reasoning, expected outcome, next steps. The reader should never have to search for the point.

## Preferred Language
improve, clarify, reduce, support, simplify, understand, collaborate, enable, explore, consider, balance, align, learn, discover, strengthen.

## Language to Avoid
leverage, synergy, game changer, move the needle, low-hanging fruit, circle back, boil the ocean, best-in-class, world-class, rockstar, ninja, disruptive, paradigm shift. Replace with plain language.

## Calibrating to the Person
The tone principles above are a default, not a fixed register. Adjust directness and warmth based on the relationship and how the specific person communicates — someone who's direct and low-context may read heavy acknowledgment as padding, while someone more relational may need more of it before the point lands. The goal is the reader feeling understood, not mechanical adherence to a formula.

## Emotional Tone
Empathetic without becoming sentimental. Show compassion through understanding and thoughtful solutions rather than emotional statements.
- Instead of: "I'm so sorry everyone is struggling."
- Write: "The current process creates unnecessary friction for the people doing the work every day."

## Checklist Before Finalizing
- Is the main point immediately clear?
- Does every recommendation include reasoning?
- Have I explained tradeoffs where they exist?
- Does this respect the reader's intelligence?
- Is the language human-centered?
- Have I removed unnecessary jargon and em-dashes?
- Does the writing sound calm and confident?
- Would this help someone make a better decision?
- Does this invite collaboration instead of shutting down discussion?
- If I were the recipient, especially on a hard day, would this land as dismissive or blaming anywhere?
- Have I acknowledged the reaction a reasonable reader would have, before asking them to move past it?
