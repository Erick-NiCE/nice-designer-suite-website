# Positioning, messaging, and storytelling

Load this when the task is specifically a positioning statement, product
messaging, or a narrative/story meant to carry an idea rather than report
findings.

## Positioning

Work through the template, then pressure-test what falls out of it — the
template is scaffolding, not the deliverable:

> For [target audience] who [problem / need], our product is
> [category / frame] that [primary value]. Unlike [alternative], we
> [meaningful differentiation].

Then ask, honestly, of the draft:

- Is the audience specific enough to picture an actual person, or could it
  be "everyone"?
- Is the problem meaningful enough that someone would change behavior to
  solve it?
- Is the value stated as an outcome, or as a feature description wearing an
  outcome's clothes?
- Is the differentiation credible given what the product actually does
  today?
- Is the claim defensible if someone asked "prove it"?
- Would a competitor plausibly say the exact same sentence about their own
  product? If yes, it isn't differentiation yet.

Avoid leaning on claims that are generic enough to apply to almost any
product in the category — *seamless, innovative, powerful, next-generation,
intuitive, best-in-class* — unless there's a concrete, specific reason to
believe attached to the claim. "Seamless" backed by "one-click setup with no
account required" is fine; "seamless" alone is filler that a reader learns
to skip past.

## Messaging

Move from feature to outcome deliberately: **feature → capability → user
benefit → outcome.** The test for good messaging is whether it answers "why
should I care?" in the reader's terms, not the product's.

- Instead of "AI-powered analytics," write "identify the accounts most
  likely to churn before the warning signs become obvious."

Translate the technical capability into the consequence a real person in
the target role would actually feel — a saved afternoon, an avoided
mistake, a decision made with more confidence than before.

## Storytelling

Use narrative structure when it genuinely improves comprehension, not as
decoration on top of a list of facts. A structure that tends to work for
product stories:

> Situation → friction → realization → insight → opportunity → future state

Make the user the protagonist of the story, not the product. The product is
what enables the transformation; the transformation — what changes for the
user — is what the reader should end up caring about. A story where the
product is the hero reads like an ad; a story where the user's situation
changes reads like a reason to act.
