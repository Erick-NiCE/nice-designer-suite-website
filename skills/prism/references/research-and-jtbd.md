# Research discipline, synthesis, and jobs-to-be-done

Load this when the task is working from real research inputs — interview
transcripts, survey exports, support tickets, sales call notes, a pile of
feedback — or when a feature request needs to be traced back to the job it's
actually serving.

## Research discipline

Search before asserting anything about the present-day world; don't rely on
memory for facts that could have changed. Prefer primary sources — company
documentation, direct user statements, regulatory text — over secondhand
summaries of them. Credible academic work, expert analysis, and high-quality
journalism all have a place; so do community sources like Reddit or forums
specifically for understanding lived experience, sentiment, and behavioral
patterns that people won't say in a formal interview.

Keep fact and interpretation visibly separate as you go, not just in the
final write-up — it's much easier to lose track of which is which later.
Flag conflicting evidence instead of quietly picking the version that fits
the narrative. State uncertainty explicitly when the evidence is thin rather
than writing around the gap. Never fabricate a statistic, a quote, a finding,
or a claim about customer behavior — a plausible-sounding invented number is
worse than an honest "we don't have data on this yet," because it gets acted
on. When you have the choice, a small number of strong, well-evidenced
insights beats a long list of weak ones — the second kind reads as thorough
but doesn't actually help anyone decide anything.

While reading through research material, watch for: recurring patterns,
contradictions between sources, behavioral differences between segments,
unmet needs, workarounds people have built for themselves, pain points,
switching behavior, moments of friction and moments of delight, unmet
expectations, triggers that start a behavior, barriers that stop it,
anxieties, motivations, contextual factors, and — often the most
revealing — the gap between what people say and what they actually do.

## Synthesizing a large pile of research

When given many sources, notes, interviews, or documents at once, work
through this sequence rather than summarizing document-by-document:

1. Extract the meaningful evidence — not everything said, the parts that
   bear on the question at hand.
2. Normalize terminology — different participants describe the same thing
   differently; don't let that create false distinctions.
3. Group into recurring themes.
4. Identify contradictions between sources or segments.
5. Separate frequency from importance — see below.
6. Identify outliers deliberately, rather than smoothing them into the
   average.
7. Distinguish symptoms from underlying causes.
8. Generate candidate insights from the grouped evidence.
9. Test each candidate insight against the evidence again — does it
   actually hold, or did it sound good in isolation?
10. Rank by strategic importance and by confidence in the evidence, which
    are different axes.
11. Translate the surviving insights into opportunities.
12. Recommend what still needs investigating or deciding next.

Frequency and importance are not the same thing — a behavior only three
people mentioned but that predicts churn matters more than one twenty
people mentioned in passing. Say explicitly when you're prioritizing a rare
but severe finding over a common minor one, so the reasoning is visible
rather than looking like an oversight.

## Jobs-to-be-done thinking

When a feature request shows up, don't take it at face value — ask what the
person is actually trying to accomplish when they ask for it.

Instead of accepting "users want a dashboard," explore: the functional job
(what task is this actually for), the emotional job (how do they want to
feel, or stop feeling), the social job (how does this affect how they're
seen by others), the trigger (what moment makes them reach for this), the
desired outcome, what they're doing today as a workaround, what they're
anxious about getting wrong, and what would make them switch away from
their current approach.

Use this framework to think, not necessarily to write — forcing "job to be
done," "trigger," and "desired outcome" into a document makes it harder to
read if the audience doesn't already speak that shorthand. The discipline
is what matters; plain language usually communicates the result better than
the vocabulary that produced it.
