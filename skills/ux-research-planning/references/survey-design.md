# Survey design

Read this when a study calls for a quantitative instrument: validating a direction across a
larger sample, testing the prevalence of a theme already found qualitatively, or building a
benchmarking instrument like SUS.

A survey is a poor tool for discovery. It can only ask about what you already thought to ask.
Use it after qualitative work has surfaced a direction, not instead of it.

## Structure

- **Context-setter.** Orient the respondent before asking anything, without leading them toward
  an answer.
- **Behavioral anchors.** Ask what people actually did before asking about attitudes or
  preferences. Behavior first, opinion second. Asking preference first contaminates the
  behavioral answers that follow.
- **Core questions.** The ones that answer the actual research question. Everything else in the
  survey exists to support these.
- **Open-ended closer.** Room for what the fixed-response questions did not anticipate. This is
  also your early warning that the instrument missed something.

## Response format, matched to what is being measured

- Frequency scales for behaviors ("how often do you...").
- Likert scales for opinions and attitudes.
- NPS for advocacy and likelihood to recommend.
- Max-diff for ranking or prioritization among several options. Use this rather than asking
  respondents to rate everything highly, which is what rating scales produce for priorities.

## Quality checks before sending

- No leading language ("don't you agree that...").
- No double-barreled questions, meaning two claims bundled into one, forcing a single answer to
  cover both.
- No jargon the respondent would not use to describe their own experience.
- A one-sentence rationale for every question: why it is here, what decision it informs. This is
  what makes the survey defensible to stakeholders, and it is also the fastest way to find the
  questions that should be cut. A question with no rationale is length you are spending on
  nothing.

## When a survey is the right call

- **Feature validation.** Pressure-testing a proposed solution against actual behavior, once
  qualitative work has surfaced a direction.
- **Concept testing.** Choosing between directions at scale.
- **Post-interview follow-up.** Testing the prevalence of a theme found in interviews across a
  larger sample. This is the highest-value use: it converts "several participants mentioned" into
  a number.

## SUS (System Usability Scale)

SUS is a fixed 10-item instrument, alternating positive and negative phrasing, scored 0 to 100:

- Odd-numbered items (1, 3, 5, 7, 9): contribution equals response minus 1.
- Even-numbered items (2, 4, 6, 8, 10): contribution equals 5 minus response.
- Sum all 10 contributions, giving a range of 0 to 40, then multiply by 2.5.

The score is not a percentage. Report it against published norms, where roughly 68 is average,
rather than reading it as a grade.

Do not modify the wording or the item count. The entire value of SUS is comparability across
studies and over time, and that depends on using the standard instrument exactly as published.
A customized SUS is an ordinary satisfaction survey with a misleading name, and it cannot be
compared against either norms or your own earlier rounds.
